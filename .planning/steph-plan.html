A calorie question that affects almost everyone

2 minutes most important

When a woman signs up she ticks what she wants and types a goal weight. If she ticks both "lose fat" and "build muscle", the app reads that as body recomposition and gives her maintenance calories, the number that keeps her exactly where she is. Then it saves the goal weight anyway and builds a plan that will never reach it.

Rodney signed up this week: 194 lb now, 174 lb goal, lose fat and build muscle. The app gave him 2,701 calories, which is exactly what keeps him at 194. He asked to lose 20 pounds. The app said yes, then made a plan that cannot get him there.

"I want to lose fat and tone up" is probably the most common way a woman describes her goal, so this will hit a lot of the women joining in September.

Why we lean toward a deficit

A typed goal weight is a specific promise; a checkbox is a mood. For your audience "build muscle" usually means "look toned". The app puts the scale in her face every week, so a number that never moves reads as failure. And a moderate deficit with high protein and lifting is recomposition for most people, and it moves the scale too.

Already fixed either way: the app used to congratulate her at signup for a flat scale, then nag her about it 14 days later with a "looks like a plateau" banner. Same number, opposite verdict. That contradiction is gone.

Decision 4 of 4

Workouts that change with her cycle

5 minutes

You asked for this. Half of it already exists, and I want to talk you out of the other half.

Already built: logging her period, working out which phase she is in, and coaching that changes with it. The coaching side already knows things like "luteal phase: cravings rise, perceived effort goes up, expect the scale to hold from water, treat that as normal rather than a setback." What does not exist is the workout itself changing.

What we found when we researched it

1. The performance effect is barely there. A 2025 review is literally titled "Evidence for Periodizing Strength Training According to Menstrual Cycle Phases Is Lacking."
2. Probably fewer than half your members have a cycle regular enough to program around, between birth control, PCOS, postpartum and perimenopause, and the app cannot tell which half.
3. This is what decided it: research on cycle apps found women given a prediction that does not match how they feel blame their own bodies, not the app.
4. But ignoring cycles is also wrong. Painful periods affect around 70% of active women, and an app that pretends otherwise loses them quietly.

So which do you want?

The bit that could set you apart

One honest page saying: symptoms are real, the evidence that your cycle changes what your muscles can build is weak, and consistency beats timing. Every competitor sells the opposite. That is a credibility position your brand can own, and it heads off the "my other app says I should rest this week" question before it arrives.
