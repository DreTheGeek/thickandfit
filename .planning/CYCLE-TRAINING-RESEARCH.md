# Should a fitness app change a woman's workout based on her menstrual cycle phase?

**Answer: Option B, and a narrow version of it.** Ship an optional, one-tap lighter swap that is triggered by what she logs today (pain, energy, sleep), not by a predicted phase. Show phase as context if she wants it, never as a prescription driver, and never as a claim about how she should be performing. Do not build C.

Everything below is the evidence for that call, including where it is weak.

---

## 1. What the evidence actually says about strength, power and recovery across the cycle

### The two anchor reviews

**McNulty et al. 2020, *Sports Medicine*** — the meta-analysis most often cited as *supporting* cycle syncing, which is not what it says.
78 studies, 1,193 women (5 to 100 per study); 51 studies / 362 effect sizes in the pairwise meta-analysis, 73 studies in the network meta-analysis.

- Early follicular vs all other phases: **ES = −0.06 [95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04]**. Trivial, and the interval crosses zero.
- Largest single contrast, early follicular vs late follicular: **ES = −0.14 [95% CrI −0.26 to −0.03]**. Still trivial by any conventional threshold.
- Quality of evidence graded **"low" for 42%** of studies, high for only 8%.
- Authors' conclusion: general guidelines cannot be formed; a personalised approach is recommended.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7497427/

For scale: an effect size of 0.14 on a 100 kg squat is roughly a 2 to 4 kg difference, well inside day-to-day noise from sleep, caffeine, food and warm-up.

**Colenso-Semple et al. 2023, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*** — umbrella review of 5 systematic reviews/meta-analyses on resistance exercise specifically.

- AMSTAR-2 scores of the underlying reviews: **4 to 7 out of 11**. Overall quality of evidence low to moderate.
- Effect sizes across all reviews: **0.01 to 0.14**, i.e. trivial throughout.
- Methodological critique is the important part: most primary studies assumed a 28-day cycle with mid-cycle ovulation, which the authors call an inaccurate generalisation; basal body temperature verification was unreliable (only ~22% of cycles showed a temperature rise coinciding with the LH surge).
- Conclusion: designing resistance training programs around cycle phase is **"not an evidence-based approach."**
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full

### The mechanistic test, done properly

**Colenso-Semple et al. 2025, *The Journal of Physiology*.** n=12, within-participant, each participant trained one leg in the follicular phase and the other in the luteal phase, counterbalanced. Phase confirmed by cycle tracking, urinary LH kits *and* serum hormones. Deuterium oxide tracer, vastus lateralis biopsies, 5-day integrated muscle protein synthesis.

- Exercise raised MPS strongly (p < 0.001).
- Phase did nothing. Follicular: 1.33%/day rest, 1.52%/day exercised. Luteal: 1.28%/day rest, 1.46%/day exercised. No effect of phase, no interaction, no phase effect on whole-body myofibrillar breakdown.
https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342

This matters because the whole "build muscle in the follicular phase" story rests on an assumed anabolic/catabolic difference. Measured directly, with the best available methods, it is not there. The accompanying *J Physiol* editorial concludes hormone fluctuations "do not compromise the capacity of women to respond effectively to an appropriate resistance exercise stimulus," and recommends autoregulating by how she feels rather than by phase.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11870042/

### Contraceptive users: same answer

**Elliott-Sale et al. 2020, *Sports Medicine*.** 42 studies, 590 participants. OC use vs non-use: ES 0.13 to 0.18, credible intervals crossing zero. **83% of studies were moderate, low or very low quality.** Conclusion: any group-level effect is likely trivial, no evidence to warrant general guidance.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7497464/

### The parallel literature on cognition, which is a useful sanity check

**PLOS One 2025 meta-analysis**: 102 articles, 3,943 participants, 730 comparisons across attention, memory, executive function, spatial and verbal ability. **No effect survived correction for multiple comparisons.** The two spatial findings that looked significant disappeared in the studies that actually verified phase with hormones. The authors explicitly flag the gap between measured null effects and strong subjective and cultural belief.
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0318576

### Where the evidence pushing the other way comes from, and why I discount it

- **Scientific Reports 2026 (Niering et al.), n=18.** Reports half-squat 1RM of 94.4 kg in late follicular/ovulation vs 87.2 kg late luteal, with **η²p = 0.98**. That effect size is not physiologically credible in an 18-person repeated-measures design; the authors themselves caution about it, and estradiol and progesterone were never measured. This is exactly the kind of study that gets screenshotted into an Instagram carousel.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-47706-0 · https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260412/Researchers-map-how-menstrual-cycle-phases-affect-athletic-performance.aspx
- **Qualitative work (Augustsson & Findhé-Malenica 2025, n=5)** finds women vividly describe feeling strongest around ovulation and hitting "a breaking point" after. Real experience, real product signal, zero evidence about physiology.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11897035/

### The most recent training-realistic study

**Sports Medicine, May 2026 (Australian Catholic University).** 28 resistance-trained women, two full supervised mesocycles, phase verified by calendar counting *plus* urinary ovulation tests *plus* retrospective serum estradiol and progesterone. Designed specifically because prior work used isolated test days rather than real training. Framing and available summaries indicate minimal effect of phase on kinematic outputs, with the interesting variance sitting in symptoms, motivation and readiness. I could not access the full results table behind the paywall, so treat my characterisation of its numbers as provisional.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02459-8

### Honest summary of section 1

Direction of effect is consistent (early follicular is very slightly worse), magnitude is trivial, evidence quality is poor, and the best-controlled mechanistic study finds nothing. **The replication picture is: the null replicates, the positive findings do not.** Nobody has run the study that would actually justify Option C, which is a matched-volume trial of phase-periodised vs non-phase-periodised programming over months with hormone verification. The 2025 *Strength & Conditioning Journal* review is titled, plainly, "Evidence for Periodizing Strength and/or Endurance Training According to Menstrual Cycle Phases to Optimize Female Athlete Performance Is Lacking."
https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2025/12000/evidence_for_periodizing_strength_and_or_endurance.4.aspx

## 2. What proportion of women even have a cycle you could program around

This is the part that kills Option C independent of the physiology.

**Cycles are not 28 days.** Bull et al. 2019, 612,613 ovulatory cycles from 124,648 users: mean length 29.3 days, and **only 13% of cycles were 28 days.** Follicular phase length varied enormously (roughly 10 to 30 days), luteal phase 7 to 17. The follicular phase is precisely the part you would need to predict to place a "peak week."
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-019-0152-7

**Irregularity is common.** Apple Women's Health Study: 16.4% showed cycle deviations, **25.6% self-reported irregular cycles**, 12.3% had diagnosed PCOS, 26.3% reported prolonged time to regularity. Variability was larger in Hispanic and Asian participants than white participants, and rose ~200% in women over 50 vs 35 to 39.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00848-1

**Hormonal contraception, US.** 54.3% of females 15 to 49 use contraception. Pill 11.4%, LARC (IUDs + implants) 10.5%, female sterilisation 11.5%, male condom 7.1%. Injectable, ring and patch are not broken out in the brief. Netting out copper IUDs and sterilisation, roughly **one in five US women of reproductive age is on a hormonal method** that suppresses or abolishes the natural cycle. Pill use is notably lower among Hispanic (9.1%) and Black (6.7%) women than white women (14.1%), which matters for this audience.
https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db539.htm · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK618450/

**Hormonal contraception, Latin America.** Modern contraceptive prevalence ranges from 31.3% (Haiti) to 79.4% (Brazil), with Colombia 76.2%, Cuba 74.9%, Paraguay 72.6%. Critically, **short-acting hormonal methods (pill and injectable) dominate the method mix in most countries**; LARC use exceeded 10% in only 6 of 23 countries. Injectable DMPA in particular produces amenorrhea in a large fraction of long-term users. For a bilingual app, the LATAM half of the audience is *more* likely to be on a hormonal method that removes a trackable cycle, not less.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6367565/

**PCOS.** WHO: 8 to 13% of reproductive-aged women, **up to 70% undiagnosed worldwide.** Roughly 70 to 75% of women with PCOS experience chronic anovulation, and anovulatory bleeding means a bleed does not confirm ovulation. So a PCOS user can log a period and get a confidently wrong phase prediction.
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/polycystic-ovary-syndrome

**Perimenopause.** Median onset 47.5, transition lasts 4 to 8 years (up to 14). The STRAW+10 *definition* of early transition is cycle-length variability greater than 7 days. Perimenopause is literally defined by the cycle becoming unpredictable.
https://journals.lww.com/menopausejournal/fulltext/2019/10000/the_menopause_transition_and_women_s_health_at.20.aspx

**Postpartum.** Exclusively breastfeeding women commonly go 4 to 18+ months without menses; fewer than 25% had menstruated by 6 months in one study, and 8 to 33% ovulate before the first bleed. No usable cycle signal for a long stretch of exactly the population Stephanie serves.
https://llli.org/breastfeeding-info/menstruation/ · https://www.fertstert.org/article/S0015-0282(16)54180-6/pdf (these are secondary/older sources; the direction is well established but I would not lean on precise percentages)

**Athletes.** Elite British track and field, n=128 over 5 years: 66% consistently regular, **30% irregular at some point, 4% amenorrheic.** A rapid review across sports found menstrual disorder prevalence ranging 0 to 61% depending on discipline; secondary amenorrhea reached 56% in cycling and 40% in triathlon.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1296189/full · https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/19/21/14243

**Prediction accuracy is the final nail.** Calendar-based app prediction of the fertile window has been reported at around 21% accuracy with errors up to six days, because most apps still assume day-14 ovulation. (I could only read secondary summaries of the Setton/Tandfonline analysis, so treat the exact figure as indicative rather than definitive.) Even Oura, which uses temperature from 42M+ nights of sleep data, markets ovulation prediction at ">96% accurate" only *after* the fact and only for cyclers it can model.
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03007995.2018.1475348 · https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-cycle-insights/

### The arithmetic for Thick & Fit

I have not seen T&F's own demographic breakdown, so this is an estimate, not a measurement. Stack hormonal contraception (~20 to 25% US, plausibly higher in parts of LATAM), PCOS (8 to 13%, most undiagnosed), perimenopause (a real slice of a 562K-follower general audience), postpartum, and general irregularity (~16 to 26%), with overlap. **A defensible planning number is that fewer than half of paying members would have a cycle regular enough and verifiable enough to program around, and the app cannot reliably tell which half.** Option C would therefore be silently wrong for the majority of the people it touches, and would be most wrong for postpartum and PCOS women, who are among the highest-intent segments.

## 3. What coaches and sports scientists actually recommend, and where they split

**The skeptical position (currently the mainstream of the exercise-science literature):**
- Colenso-Semple: phase-based resistance training prescription is not evidence-based; individualise instead. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full
- Minahan (*J Physiol* editorial, 2025): simplify programming, autoregulate by feel, train consistently through the luteal phase, concerns about progesterone impairing adaptation are unfounded. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11870042/
- *Strength & Conditioning Journal* 2025: period-periodised training confers no additional benefit over traditional approaches. https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2025/12000/evidence_for_periodizing_strength_and_or_endurance.4.aspx
- Elliott-Sale and colleagues: the field's methods are the problem. Only 8% and 17% of studies in the two sister meta-analyses were high quality; calendar-based phase classification is inadequate; researchers should use language matching the methods they actually used. https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/ovarian-hormones-101-how-to-conduct-high-quality-sport-and-exercise-science-research-on-female-athletes · https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2025.2583013

**The pro-syncing position:**
- Stacy Sims argues a lack of cohesive strong evidence is not the same as no evidence, that sport-science studies are underpowered and male-designed, and that you should also read cell biology, endocrinology and elite case studies. Her actual prescriptions are more modest than her popular reputation: track your own cycle, raise protein (~12%) and carbohydrate in the luteal phase, avoid *overreaching* in the late luteal phase. She explicitly frames this as optimisation, not limitation, and says women can compete on any day of the cycle. Her strongest point, and it is a good one: the reviews exclude women with menstrual disorders, yet 74% of athletes report dysmenorrhea and 78% report premenstrual symptoms, so the reviews systematically exclude the people most likely to benefit. https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/Cutting_Through_the_Confusion_of_Cycle-Sync_Style_Training
- There is a live 2025 public debate between Sims and Colenso-Semple, which is a fair signal that this is contested territory rather than settled. https://docswholift.com/podcast/womens-fitness-needs-debate-dr-stacey-sims-vs-dr-lauren-colenso-semple/
- A 2025 *S&C Journal* paper argues for phase-based S&C in elite team sport specifically. I hit a paywall and could not read it, so I flag it as existing rather than characterise it. https://www.ovid.com/jnls/nsca-scj/abstract/10.1519/ssc.0000000000000879~menstrual-cycle-phase-based-strength-and-conditioning

**Where they actually agree, which is what you should build on:** nobody credible recommends a fixed calendar-driven program. The skeptics say autoregulate by feel. Sims says track *your own* response. Both are describing symptom-responsive training, not phase-predicted training. That agreement is your product spec.

I also tried to read the Stronger by Science practitioner guide on this topic and got a 403, so I am not attributing a position to them.

## 5. The risk side: what goes wrong when an app tells her how she should be performing

**Prediction becomes experience.** The strongest direct evidence I found is an HCI study at DIS '26 (Zhou, Karaturhan, Weilenmann, Zhu), titled after a participant's own words: *"It became a self-fulfilling prophecy."* Users report that app mood and symptom predictions shape how they subsequently perceive their bodies, with documented anxiety and **self-blame when their body diverges from the prediction.**
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13261

**Users blame their bodies, not the algorithm.** A European qualitative study of menstrual tracking app users found tracking produced calm in some and distress in others, generated anxiety by making users brace for an upcoming period unnecessarily, made them question the quality of their health, and specifically found a tendency to **perceive their own bodies as faulty rather than question the app's accuracy.** That is the exact failure mode of Option C: an unverifiable prediction that the user will resolve against herself.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-019-7549-8

**The perception/reality gap is enormous and it moves behaviour.** 76.8% of elite British track and field athletes said their cycle negatively affected performance, against a literature showing trivial measured effects. A 2024 scoping review of 39 studies found the proportion of athletes reporting negative impact ranged from **2.8% to 100%** across studies, which tells you the number is measuring beliefs and question wording, not physiology.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1296189/full · https://www.jsams.org/article/S1440-2440(24)00077-X/fulltext

**Cycle narratives already push women out of exercise.** 84% of teenage girls say their interest in sport and fitness declined after starting their period; 78% of previously sporty girls avoided sport during their period; 23% of adult women say the cycle is a barrier to more physical activity; nearly 60% of surveyed women felt teenage avoidance damaged their long-term relationship with exercise. An app that says "rest this week" is not neutral advice dropped into a vacuum. It lands on top of a lifetime of that message.
https://www.nuffieldhealth.com/article/menstrual-cycle-impact-on-physical-activity · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12142975/

**The cognition literature is a warning shot about legitimising the frame.** The PLOS One meta-analysis explicitly notes its results give no scientific foundation for restricting women's activities based on cycle phase, while acknowledging the cultural belief is widespread. Every "your body is in catabolic mode this week" screen is a product endorsing that frame in Stephanie's voice.
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0318576

**Data risk is not theoretical, and it is worse for this audience.** Cycle data in a US consumer app is not HIPAA-protected. The FTC found Flo shared sensitive health data with Facebook and Google despite promising not to, settling in 2021; in 2025 a California jury found Meta violated state privacy law over reproductive health data received from Flo via ad software. Post-Dobbs, cycle logs are a live legal-exposure category in several US states.
https://stateline.org/2024/07/26/data-privacy-after-dobbs-is-period-tracking-safe/ · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12131320/

For Thick & Fit specifically that means: if you collect cycle data, it must not flow to GoHighLevel, PostHog, OpenRouter or any third party, it needs its own consent, its own retention policy and its own delete path, and it should probably never leave Supabase. Feeding a member's cycle phase into an LLM prompt on OpenRouter would be the single riskiest line of code in the product.

**The counter-risk, which is real and which pure Option A ignores:** dysmenorrhea affects 68 to 74% of athletes and PMS around 78%. If the app pretends the cycle does not exist, women in pain quietly skip sessions and eventually churn. Adherence, not physiology, is the actual business case here.

## 6. Recommendation

### Not A alone
Phase education with zero product response is Clue's position, and it is scientifically defensible. But Clue is a tracker; Thick & Fit is a coaching product people pay for. Doing nothing when a member logs severe cramps is a coaching failure that the evidence does not require. The symptoms are real even though the phase effect is not.

### Not C, and not later either
Five independent reasons, any one of which is sufficient:
1. **No effect to periodise around.** Trivial effect sizes, poor-quality evidence, and a direct mechanistic null on muscle protein synthesis.
2. **No trial supports the intervention.** Nobody has tested phase-periodised vs matched non-phase programming and shown a benefit. The 2025 S&C review says so in its title.
3. **You cannot predict the input.** 13% of cycles are 28 days; the follicular phase varies by ~20 days; calendar prediction accuracy is poor.
4. **Most of this audience is not modellable.** Hormonal contraception, PCOS, postpartum, perimenopause and general irregularity, and the app cannot detect which users it is silently wrong for.
5. **Automatic changes convert a wrong guess into a body-shaming experience.** The user blames herself, not the model.

Plus a sixth, commercial: WHOOP and Wild.AI already occupy this position, one of them on a mechanism claim that current evidence contradicts. Following them means inheriting their liability without their distribution.

### Do B, specified tightly

**Trigger on logged state, not predicted phase.** The swap fires when she logs cramps, poor sleep, low energy or high soreness, on any day of any month, whether or not she tracks a cycle at all. This is autoregulation, which is what *both* camps in section 3 actually recommend, and it works identically for a woman on the injectable, a woman with PCOS, a woman four months postpartum and a woman in perimenopause. It is also the only version that keeps working when the cycle model is wrong.

**Phase is optional context, shown only if she opts in, and framed as observation not prescription.** "You logged low energy in this stretch last two months" is fine and true. "You're in your luteal phase so expect to be weaker" is a nocebo with a UI.

**The swap must preserve the training stimulus.** Same movement pattern, reduced demand: drop the top set, cut one working set, swap barbell hip thrust for dumbbell hip thrust, keep the rest. Do **not** replace strength work with yoga or rest. The MyFLO/WHOOP pattern of downgrading a whole week to restorative work is where a defensible feature turns into the message that pushed 84% of teenage girls out of sport in the first place.

**Always one tap, always reversible, never pre-applied.** The default program stays exactly as written until she taps. No auto-swap, no "we've adjusted your week for you."

**Never claim she should feel a certain way.** Every string gets reviewed against the voice bible on one test: does this tell her what her body is doing, or ask her what her body is doing? Ask, always.

**One honest education page, in both languages.** Say plainly: symptoms are real, the evidence that phase changes what your muscles can build is weak, and consistency beats timing. That page is a genuine differentiator against every competitor above and is exactly the credibility position Stephanie's brand can own. It also inoculates against the inevitable "my other app says I should rest this week" support ticket.

**Instrument it.** Log every offer, accept and ignore. That telemetry is the only thing that could ever earn a stronger feature later, and right now nobody in the market has it.

**Ship data governance with it, not after.** Separate consent, Supabase-only, no third-party egress, no LLM prompt injection of cycle data, explicit delete. Given the Flo/Meta outcome, treat cycle data as the most sensitive column in the database.

## 7. What would have to be true to change my mind

**To move me to C:**
1. A well-powered RCT (n ≥ 100, at least 2 hormone-verified cycles per participant, 12+ weeks) showing phase-periodised programming beats volume-matched non-phase programming on strength or hypertrophy. Not a difference between phases: a difference in *outcomes from programming to them*. This study does not currently exist.
2. Demonstrated **within-person reproducibility**: the same woman, same phase, same direction and magnitude of effect across at least three verified cycles, with the effect exceeding day-to-day measurement noise. Without this, "personalise to her response" is fitting noise.
3. Phase prediction validated **in our own user base** against LH or temperature, hitting something like ovulation within ±1 day for >80% of tracked cycles, with an explicit and honest "we cannot model your cycle" state for everyone else.
4. Our own B-feature telemetry showing acceptance clusters by predicted phase rather than by logged symptoms. If acceptance tracks symptoms, the phase model is adding nothing and C is a more expensive way to do B.

I would want 1 through 3 before touching C. Point 4 alone is suggestive, not sufficient.

**To move me back to A (i.e. pull B):**
- If, over 8 to 12 weeks, members offered the swap show lower session completion or lower total volume-load than a matched control, then the feature is licensing quitting rather than preventing it, and it should be removed.

**To move me further toward B+ (surfacing phase more prominently):**
- If the optional phase-context view has high voluntary opt-in and high retention among opt-ins, and support volume about cycle anxiety does not rise, then more contextual surfacing is earned. Still not prescription.

## 8. Where the evidence is weak, stated plainly

- **Hypertrophy over real timescales is basically unstudied.** Almost everything is acute performance or short interventions. The MPS study is the best mechanistic evidence available and it is n=12 over days, not months.
- **Nothing exists on the populations this app serves most.** I found no training-periodisation evidence at all for postpartum, PCOS or perimenopausal women. The reviews explicitly exclude women with menstrual disorders, which is Sims' single strongest criticism and it is correct.
- **Symptom-based prescription is untested too.** My recommendation for B rests on adherence logic and expert consensus about autoregulation, not on a trial showing symptom-triggered swaps improve outcomes. It is the low-risk option, not the proven one.
- **Nobody has directly tested the harm I am arguing against.** The self-fulfilling-prophecy finding is qualitative HCI research, and the perception/reality gap in the cognition meta-analysis is inferential. I am reasoning about mechanism, not citing a trial of "app told women they'd be weak and they were."
- **LATAM-specific data is thin.** The Lancet Global Health contraception data is from surveys since 2004 and I found no cycle-characteristics data specific to Latin American women. The claim that LATAM users are *more* likely to be on cycle-suppressing methods follows from the method mix but is an inference.
- **Sources I could not read in full**, so treat my characterisation as provisional: the *Sports Medicine* 2026 ACU study results table (paywall), the 2025 *S&C Journal* pro-phase paper (paywall), the Stronger by Science practitioner guide (403), Flo Health's current feature set (truncated page), and the Tandfonline ovulation-prediction accuracy paper (403, the 21% figure is from secondary summaries).
- **One claim I am confident is wrong and should not be copied**: WHOOP's statement that the follicular phase lets you build more muscle than other phases. That is the exact hypothesis Colenso-Semple et al. 2025 tested with the strongest available methods and did not find.
